# Writing patterns

Adapted from Humanizer's 26-pattern guide. Use these as editing diagnostics,
not proof of machine authorship or a mechanical ban on vocabulary. A purposeful
contrast, list, heading, or repeated phrase may be the clearest choice.

## Staging instead of stating

1. **Empty contrasts.** Phrases such as "not just X, but Y" often inflate an
   ordinary point. State the positive claim directly unless the contrast
   corrects a real misconception or both halves carry distinct information.
   Check contrasts split across sentences too.
2. **Repeated closers and dramatic fragments.** Cut a line that restates what
   the reader just learned, such as "That is the real win." Keep a short
   sentence when it contributes a new fact or consequence. Combine fragments
   when their only purpose is emphasis.
3. **Aphorisms without substance.** Replace "at its core" or an abstract
   "X is the language of Y" claim with the specific relationship supported by
   the text. Do not invent a relationship to make the sentence concrete.
4. **Staged openings.** Remove introductions such as "Let's dive in" or
   "Here's the thing" when the next sentence can simply make the point.
   Preserve an ordinary conversational aside when it belongs to the voice.
5. **Unraised objections.** Remove defenses against positions no one took.
   Retain a meaningful alternative or an attributed objection the text answers.

## Rhythm applied by formula

6. **Forced groups of three.** Check whether each item adds information.
   Merge redundant examples; keep all three when they are three real facts.
   Never discard a substantive item just to disrupt the rhythm.
7. **Repeated openings.** Merge adjacent sentences with the same subject or
   vary the construction when repetition is accidental. Preserve deliberate
   parallelism that helps meaning or voice.
8. **Dashes everywhere.** Prefer a period, comma, colon, or parentheses when
   it makes the relationship clearer. Follow an explicit style request or
   writing sample that uses dashes. Leave code, URLs, quoted text, and numeric
   ranges intact.
9. **Stacked qualifications.** Reduce "could potentially possibly" to the
   qualification actually supported. Retain necessary uncertainty, scope, and
   distinctions between observation, inference, and proposal.
10. **Unnecessary compound hyphens.** Use ordinary grammar for compound
    modifiers, preserving established technical terms and dictionary spellings.
    This alone is a weak reason to rewrite a passage.
11. **Passive or missing subjects.** Name the actor when known and useful.
    Do not invent an actor merely to convert passive voice to active voice.

## Inflated claims and borrowed authority

12. **Stock vocabulary.** Repeated words such as "delve," "tapestry,"
    "pivotal," or "showcase" can hide a simple point. Replace them with
    precise ordinary language when warranted; keep technical uses and names.
13. **Inflated significance.** Remove unsupported claims of transformation,
    legacy, importance, or a bright future. A stock "challenges and outlook"
    section can usually end at its last supported fact. Preserve real plans
    and consequences stated in the source.
14. **Vague associations.** Say whether someone founded, worked for, funded,
    or advised an organization only when the source establishes that role.
    Otherwise retain the uncertainty of the original relationship.
15. **Superficial analysis.** A trailing "highlighting the importance of..."
    often restates a fact rather than explaining it. Cut empty commentary;
    retain an actual mechanism or supported consequence.
16. **Promotional wording.** Replace generic praise or sales language with
    supported characteristics. Honor a marketing brief without fabricating
    superiority, demand, impact, or customer satisfaction.
17. **Vague authority and notability claims.** Replace "experts say" with an
    available named source, or preserve the attribution gap. Do not invent
    experts, quotes, citations, or endorsement. Keep substantive coverage and
    audience figures when they matter to the requested text.
18. **Avoiding simple verbs.** Prefer "is," "are," or "has" over a longer
    phrase when the meaning is identical. A verb such as "operates" may carry
    real information; do not replace it blindly.

## Formatting applied by formula

19. **Decorative bold labels.** Remove redundant labels or emphasis that adds
    no navigational value. Turn a list into prose only when it improves reading;
    retain tables, accessible structure, and task-required fields.
20. **Decorative headings.** Prefer descriptive headings and sentence case.
    Remove repeated titles, unnecessary rules, or decorative symbols unless
    the user's style or the document's purpose calls for them.
21. **Mismatched quotation marks.** Match the target format's typography.
    Curly quotes are common in human writing and are weak evidence by
    themselves. Do not alter syntax inside code or literal data.

## Chat and draft leftovers

22. **Chat wrappers.** Remove praise, greetings, offers to continue, and
    "I hope this helps" from a standalone deliverable when they are residue
    from its creation. Retain intentional salutations and sign-offs in letters.
23. **Knowledge disclaimers followed by guesses.** Remove irrelevant model
    self-description and unsupported biographical or factual guesses. Keep
    meaningful as-of dates, source limitations, and uncertainty. Missing
    evidence is not evidence that someone is private or keeps a low profile.
24. **Repeating the heading.** Cut the first sentence under a heading when it
    merely restates that heading without adding content.
25. **Narrating the document.** Cut obvious descriptions of a table, layout,
    or drafting process. Preserve provenance, methodology, change history,
    limitations, and conventions when the reader needs them to judge or act.

## Writing for the reader

26. **Rebuilding known context.** In a reply, lead with the decision and the
    reason that could change it. Avoid retelling background the recipient
    already supplied. In a standalone document, retain the context its new
    reader needs; do not assume they saw the conversation.

## Examples and final check

"It's not just a cache; it's a revolution in performance" can become "The
cache reduces repeated work" only if the source establishes that effect. With
no supporting information, remove the exaggeration without inventing a benefit.

"She noted the door. She noted its lock" can become "She noted the door and
its lock." Both observations survive the rhythm change.

"It could potentially possibly fail under load" can become "It may fail under
load." Changing it to "It fails under load" would overstate the source.

Before returning the rewrite, compare it against the original for lost claims,
added specifics, altered numbers, attribution changes, and changed certainty.
Preserve the writer's distinctive supported details, humor, ambivalence, and
intentional quirks. A natural voice does not require fabricated anecdotes.
